Most small businesses think of remote IT support as a screen sharing session. Someone is having a problem. They call. The IT person remotes in, sees what they see, fixes it, and disconnects. That is real support, and for one user with one problem at one moment, it works.

The trouble is that this is roughly five percent of what IT is supposed to do. The other ninety-five percent is the work that happens when nobody has called and nobody has noticed there is a problem yet. That work cannot be done through screen sharing because screen sharing requires a user to initiate the session. Remote management does not.

The questions remote management can answer that screen sharing cannot are the ones most businesses do not realize they should be asking. How many Macs do you actually own? Which of them are running an unsupported version of macOS? Which devices have not checked in for the last thirty days because they are sitting in a drawer? Which laptops still have FileVault disk encryption disabled despite the policy? How long is it taking, on average, to apply a critical security patch across the fleet?

A help desk that operates entirely through screen sharing has no way to answer any of these questions. A managed remote platform answers all of them as a normal part of its day.

## The asset inventory problem

The most underrated thing remote management provides is just knowing what you have. For a ten-person agency it sounds trivial. By thirty staff, it becomes a real problem. By fifty, "how many Macs do we own and where are they" is the question that costs the most when nobody can answer it. A managed platform tracks every device continuously: serial number, last user, OS version, installed apps, hardware health, security posture. That inventory is what makes everything else possible.

## What "remote management" actually means in practice

A modern managed remote setup combines three layers. Mobile Device Management (MDM) configures devices and enforces policy. A remote monitoring and management platform (RMM) watches the fleet continuously and acts on alerts. A ticketing system tracks what is happening across the fleet, who is working on what, and what has been resolved. When the three are wired together properly, the result is the experience users describe when they say "things just work." The IT does not feel absent. It feels invisible.

## When to think about this

You are due for this conversation if your IT person spends their day on the same repetitive fixes, if onboarding a new hire takes more than an hour of hands-on work, if you cannot answer how many Macs you own without counting, or if security incidents reach you through a news article before they reach you through an alert. Each one is a sign that your IT is running on screen sharing when it should be running on remote management.

The two are not interchangeable. One handles the moment a user notices a problem. The other handles everything before and after.
